# Decoding Liquid Latex Bottle Labels

> **Label literacy, not medical advice.** Food-contact rubber tables and industrial concentrate specs do not certify fashion-garment wear clearance. See [Allergy and Skin Contact](/literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact).

## Executive summary

Consumer “liquid latex” bottles rarely print the fields industrial buyers expect on **concentrate** specs. Decode ammonia band, total solids, prevulcanized versus raw, and polymer family. Marketing words like “body safe” or “FDA” are not those fields.

ASTM D1076 and ISO 2004 define ammonia-preserved NR **concentrate** categories. They **do not apply to compounded** hobby bottles.

Use this when choosing a bottle, comparing two brands, or when a seller cites food-contact or medical glove language. Pair with [Forming film from liquid latex](/literature-reviews/liquid-film-pathway) and [Liquid painting vs compounding pigment](/literature-reviews/liquid-painting-vs-compounding-pigment).

## Label fields that mean something {#label-fields}

ASTM **D1076** covers concentrated, ammonia-stabilized NR latex categories and **does not apply to compounded** concentrates. ISO **2004** specifies ammonia-preserved centrifuged or creamed concentrate types including HA, LA, and MA bands, with copper and manganese limits on total solids.

<details>
<summary>What are HA, LA, and MA ammonia bands?</summary>

ISO 2004 types include **high-ammonia (HA)**, **low-ammonia (LA)**, and **medium-ammonia (MA)** centrifuged or creamed concentrate, with copper and manganese caps on total solids. Effective preservation typically needs **more than 0.35%** ammonia. Long storage often runs **0.6–1.0%** by weight. LA systems may pair reduced ammonia with a secondary package such as TMTD / ZnO / lauric. Without an ammonia number on the TDS, you cannot place the bottle on that map.

</details>

A craft bottle that omits total solids content (TSC), ammonia %, mechanical stability time (MST), and polymer family is not auditable against those specs.

**Prevulcanized latex** is vulcanized in the **liquid** state. A proper prevulc deposit can approach conventional post-dried properties. Overcure yields short, weak rubber. Read the bottle and TDS before you assume dry-only finishes the film. See [Prevulcanized consumer latex](/literature-reviews/liquid-prevulcanized-consumer-latex).

Fresh latex coagulates within hours without preservation. Ammonia is the primary preservative in NR concentrate.

## Marketing words that are not specs {#marketing-words}

| Label phrase | What it is not |
| --- | --- |
| “FDA approved” on a bottle | Usually food-contact ingredient tables (21 CFR **177.2600**) or unrelated device claims. Not catsuit wear clearance. |
| “Medical grade” | Medical **device** rules (for example 801.437) apply to regulated devices. |
| “Body safe / skin safe” | No universal standard in the industrial specs used here. |
| “100% latex” | May mean NR, synthetic, or acrylic theatrical product. Read polymer family on the TDS. |

Do not assume every bottle is prevulcanized NR because it dries tack-free.

## Deep dives (optional)

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Asking for ammonia the useful way</summary>

Ask vendors for **ammonia as NH₃ on wet latex**. Vanderbilt reports NH₃ on wet latex, water phase, or dry rubber. Example: 62% latex at 0.65% NH₃ wet. Ventilation needs scale with HA versus LA. See [Liquid ventilation and ammonia](/literature-reviews/liquid-ventilation-ammonia).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Prevulcanized vs post-cure on a label</summary>

Prevulcanized NR films peak tensile at a lower modulus than postvulcanized films because high crosslink can limit particle integration on drying. Gloves from prevulcanized latex need only warm-air drying. Post-form oven vulcanization of dried compound is a separate branch. Do not merge the branches from a marketing blurb.

</details>
