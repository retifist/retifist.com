# Decoding Consumer Latex Bottle Labels: Concentrate Fields vs Marketing

> **Label literacy, not medical advice.** Food-contact rubber tables and industrial concentrate specs do **not** certify fashion-garment wear clearance. See [Allergy and Skin Contact](/literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact) for allergy classes.

## Executive summary

Consumer “liquid latex” bottles rarely print the same fields industrial buyers expect on **concentrate** specs. **Liquid latex work** shoppers need to decode ammonia band, total solids, prevulcanized vs raw, and polymer family — not marketing words like “body safe” or “FDA.” ASTM D1076 and ISO 2004 define ammonia-preserved NR **concentrate** categories; they **do not apply to compounded** hobby bottles.

Use this when choosing a liquid-latex bottle, comparing two brands, or when a seller cites food-contact or medical glove language out of scope. Pair with [Sheet vs Liquid Film Pathways](/literature-reviews/sheet-vs-liquid-film-pathways) and [Liquid Latex Painting vs Compounding Pigment](/literature-reviews/liquid-latex-painting-vs-compounding-pigment).

## Label fields that actually mean something {#label-fields}

ASTM **D1076** covers concentrated, ammonia-stabilized NR latex categories and **does not apply to compounded** concentrates. ISO **2004** specifies ammonia-preserved centrifuged/creamed concentrate types including HA, LA, and MA bands with Cu/Mn limits on total solids.

<details>
<summary>What are HA, LA, and MA ammonia bands?</summary>

ISO 2004 types include **high-ammonia (HA)**, **low-ammonia (LA)**, and **medium-ammonia (MA)** centrifuged/creamed concentrate with copper and manganese caps on total solids. Effective preservation typically needs **>0.35%** ammonia; long storage often **0.6–1.0%** by weight. Without an ammonia number on the TDS, you cannot place the bottle on the map.

</details>

A craft bottle that omits total solids content (TSC), ammonia %, mechanical stability time (MST), and polymer family is not auditable against those specs.

**Prevulcanized latex** is vulcanized in the **liquid** state; a proper prevulcanized deposit can approach conventional post-dried properties; overcure yields short, weak rubber. Read the bottle/TDS before assuming dry-only finishes your garment film.

Fresh latex coagulates within hours without preservation; ammonia is the primary preservative in NR concentrate.

## Marketing words that are not specs {#marketing-words}

| Label phrase | What it is **not** |
| --- | --- |
| “FDA approved” on a bottle | Usually food-contact ingredient tables (21 CFR **177.2600**) or unrelated device claims — not catsuit wear clearance |
| “Medical grade” | Medical **device** rules (e.g. 801.437) apply to regulated devices |
| “Body safe / skin safe” | No universal standard in industrial specs |
| “100% latex” | May mean NR, synthetic, or acrylic theatrical product — read polymer family on TDS |

Do not assume every bottle is prevulcanized NR because it dries tack-free.

## Deep dives (optional)

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: HA / LA / MA ammonia bands</summary>

Ask vendors for **ammonia as NH₃ on wet latex**. Vanderbilt reports NH₃ on wet latex, water phase, or dry rubber — example: 62% latex at 0.65% NH₃ wet. Ventilation needs scale with HA vs LA — see [Home Shop Ventilation](/literature-reviews/home-shop-ventilation-solvent-exposure).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Prevulcanized vs post-cure branch</summary>

Prevulcanized NR films peak tensile at **lower modulus** than postvulcanized films because high crosslink can limit **particle integration** on drying. Gloves from prevulcanized latex need only **warm air drying**. Post-form oven vulcanization of dried compound is a separate branch — do not merge the branches from a marketing blurb alone.

</details>
