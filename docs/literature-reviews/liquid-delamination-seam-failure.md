# Liquid Delamination and Seam Failure

## Executive summary

Read the broken join before you add more latex. **Adhesive failure** leaves glue on one side only. **Cohesive failure** splits the glue layer. On a **multi-dip** piece, layers can come apart from each other even when there was no separate cement: that is a **ply split**, usually timing or humidity, not a missing extra coat.

ASTM peel methods are lab classes. Use this with [Liquid adhesives](/literature-reviews/liquid-adhesives-and-seam-integrity) and [Liquid reinforcement](/literature-reviews/liquid-reinforcement-laminate-zones).

---

## Peel modes {#peel-modes}

<details>
<summary>What is adhesive vs cohesive failure?</summary>

**Adhesive failure:** the glue let go from one surface. That face looks clean. **Cohesive failure:** the glue itself tore. Both sides show residue.

</details>

| Mode | What you see | Likely class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adhesive | Glue on one piece only | Prep, contamination, polarity, never wetted |
| Cohesive | Glue split through its thickness | Weak film, slow dry, restricted coalescence, water |
| Fiber-pull | Textile fibers stuck in the glue | Textile side lost |
| Rubber-tear | Film tears away from the join | Film weaker than the bond |
| Ply split | Dip layers separate from each other | Underlayer dried or leached; low humidity |

Peel **rate** can flip cohesive and adhesive in the pressure-sensitive-adhesive study cited for this series. A slow hand peel is not a fast wear yank.

---

## Multi-dip ply split {#ply-split}

Bond strength between dips drops when the first layer is **dried and leached** before the next dip. Fresh **wet gel** is the window that maximizes adhesion. Fatty soaps (handbook example: potassium caprylate) hurt inter-ply adhesion. Low relative humidity can impair second-dip lamination.

If the hood or glove peels between coats, that is this class. See [Liquid reinforcement](/literature-reviews/liquid-reinforcement-laminate-zones).

## Decision flow {#decision-flow}

```text
1. Split the join — look at both faces
2. Classify mode (adhesive / cohesive / fiber / rubber / ply split)
3. Location? edge vs mid-panel
4. Age? fresh vs months
5. Fix class: re-prep vs wet-gel timing vs redesign the lap vs retire
```

Glue family: [Liquid adhesives](/literature-reviews/liquid-adhesives-and-seam-integrity). Textile face: [Liquid latex–textile bonding](/literature-reviews/liquid-latex-textile-bonding). Rim coats: [Liquid edge finishing](/literature-reviews/liquid-edge-finishing-materials).

## Aged aqueous adhesive {#aged-failure}

Latex adhesives can be slow drying. Crosslinking improves temperature, ageing, water, and solvent resistance of the bond, and it cuts tack. In water-based PSA, a sulfur-donor cure aged better than colloidal free sulfur. Heat and oxidation can soften or embrittle films.

NR latex adhesive retains a high molecular-weight fraction that solvent-milled NR does not. That tape study is not a garment bake-off; it only says the two families are not the same film.

## Deep dives (optional)

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: ASTM peel method classes</summary>

ASTM D903: peel/stripping of flexible bonds. ASTM D1876: T-peel, flexible-to-flexible. Report failure mode with force. Lab classes, not a garment grade.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Multi-dip window</summary>

Inter-ply bond drops when the first layer is dried and leached; fresh wet gel maximizes adhesion. Remedies in that industrial account: minimize underlayer drying and leaching; avoid excess fatty soaps.

</details>
