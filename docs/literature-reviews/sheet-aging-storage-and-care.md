# Sheet Aging, Storage and Care

## Executive summary

Heat, light, ozone, oils, and a crease that sits for months wreck **calendered** sheet faster than the date on the roll. Storage slows that clock. It does not stop it.

<details>
<summary>What does calendered mean?</summary>

**Calendered** sheet is rubber squeezed between heavy, heated rollers to an even thickness with smooth faces. Fashion sheet from a supplier is almost always this: a formed film you cut and glue.

</details>

Use this when you have unused stock or a finished garment you plan to wear again. You get the [drivers](#why-sheet-ages), a [closet checklist](#storage-checklist), four [symptom classes](#aging-field-guide), and a [hard fence against oils](#care-level-fence).

---

## Why sheet ages {#why-sheet-ages}

The environment attacks the film. The handbook lists heat, humidity, ultraviolet light, high-energy radiation, ozone, oxygen, chemicals (acids, bases, oils, solvents, oxidizers, heavy metals), and stress.

Oxidation does three things at once. The face can go soft and tacky (the chains break). It can harden and go brittle (**crosslinking**). It can change how it dissolves. Light speeds the oxygen reaction. Once it starts, it feeds itself.

<details>
<summary>What is crosslinking?</summary>

**Crosslinking** ties long rubber molecules into a network. Extra links from aging make rubber hard and brittle instead of stretchy. Chain scission is the opposite: the surface goes soft and sticky.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What is ozone?</summary>

**Ozone** (O₃) is a reactive form of oxygen. It forms from electrical discharge (motors, fluorescent or mercury lamps, sparking equipment), from lightning, and from ultraviolet light plus nitrogen oxides in city air. Ozone cracks run **across** the stretch, often over a wide area. Flex cracks stay in the spot that was strained. UV damage has no preferred direction.

</details>

**Fatty-acid salts** of copper, cobalt, manganese, and iron speed that oxidation. The handbook names those metal soaps as catalysts.

Oils, greases, and fuels swell vulcanized rubber and cut strength. That is a measured test class.

| Driver | What you may notice | What to change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heat | Tack or hardening sooner than you expected | Cool room. Do not park rolls against a hot ceiling or a radiator. |
| UV / light | Dull, discolored, or crazed face | Dark storage. Faces sitting in plastic or cellophane that see UV will discolor. |
| Ozone | Fine cracks across a stretched area, often perpendicular to the pull | Keep ozone-generating equipment out of the room. See [Storage](#storage-checklist). |
| Oxygen | Slow aging even in the dark | Slow it. Do not expect to stop it. |
| Oils / solvents | Swell, softness, loss of strength | Keep petroleum, mineral oil, and oil lotions off the film. See [Care-level](#care-level-fence). |
| Continuous fold | A crease that becomes a crack line | Do not leave a knife-edge fold sitting for months. |

## Storage checklist {#storage-checklist}

Keep the room **cool, dark, and ozone-aware**.

**ISO 2230** is a storage framework for finished rubber products: inspect, record, package, protect. It is not a fashion-care manual. Sample text: protect from circulating air when you can; keep ozone-generating equipment out of the room (fluorescent and mercury lamps, high-voltage gear, electric motors, sparking equipment); keep out combustion gases and organic vapours that can make ozone in light.

<details>
<summary>What is ISO 2230?</summary>

**ISO 2230** is an international standard for storing vulcanized or thermoplastic rubber **products**. It covers inspection, recording, packaging, and storage. It is a product-storage framework, not a wash chapter.

</details>

One vendor card aligned to that standard (not the standard itself) says: below 25 °C, no sun, ozone-aware, store without tensile stress.

Non-air-conditioned warehouses run much hotter near the ceiling than the floor. Goods against the ceiling age faster. Do not store sheet against ceiling heat.

### Unused sheet and finished garments {#unused-sheet-and-garments}

Surfaces of latex goods packed in plastic or cellophane that see ultraviolet light will discolor.

The handbook talks about **clay-board boxes**: the old industrial cardboard cartons used to ship latex goods. Those cartons are not sealed against air. The packing rule is about the fold, not the brand of box. Do not pack the film so a sharp edge sits in the carton. The handbook example is latex girdles rolled (no sharp crease) into cardboard tubes.

Keep rolls and garments out of sun, away from motors and fluorescent or mercury lamps, and without a knife-edge crease sitting for months.

## Aging field guide {#aging-field-guide}

Name the **class** first. Wax bloom is additive moving to the surface. It is not dirt.

| Class | How it looks | What you can do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bloom | Haze or film, can look like dust | Watch it. Do not scrape it as a “clean.” |
| Sticky tack | Soft, tacky face from oxidative chain scission | Cut heat and light. Oil “conditioners” are the wrong family. See [Care-level](#care-level-fence). Industrial chlorination can cut tack. That is not a kitchen recipe. |
| Ozone crack | Cracks perpendicular to stress, often widespread | Stop stretching the damaged zone. Polish does not close a crack. Patch vs retire is a glue question: [Sheet adhesives and seam integrity](/literature-reviews/sheet-adhesives-and-seam-integrity). |
| UV dull / crazing | Surface crazing with no preferred direction; discolor on UV-exposed packaged faces | Dark storage from here on. |

Oven heat-aging is a lab method class. It is not a closet protocol.

## Care-level fence {#care-level-fence}

Do not use oil-based hand creams or lotions with latex gloves unless the product is shown to keep barrier protection. Petroleum-based lubricants have been noted to deteriorate latex gloves. Oils, greases, and fuels deteriorate vulcanized rubber as a test class.

Keep petroleum, mineral oil, petrolatum, and oil lotions off the sheet. Do not “feed” a garment with oil.

A small patch vs retire is a **glue-family** question. See [Sheet adhesives and seam integrity](/literature-reviews/sheet-adhesives-and-seam-integrity). Solvent-cement patches are flammable liquids: closed containers, away from heat and sparks. Read the **product** SDS. Shop vapor rules live in [Sheet ventilation and solvent exposure](/literature-reviews/sheet-ventilation-solvent-exposure).

<details>
<summary>What is an SDS?</summary>

An **SDS** (Safety Data Sheet) is the manufacturer’s hazard and handling document. It lists flammability, ventilation, first aid, and storage. For a solvent cement, the SDS on the can beats forum advice.

</details>

Industrial mildewcides protect NR films in plant settings. That is not a skin-contact wearable recipe. Skin-reaction literacy: [Allergy and Skin Contact](/literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact).

## Safety {#safety}

- **Oils and petroleum** attack natural-rubber film. See [Care-level](#care-level-fence).
- **Ozone:** keep fluorescent and mercury lamps, motors, high-voltage gear, and sparking equipment out of storage. See [Storage](#storage-checklist).
- **Solvent patches:** flammable. Ventilate. No sparks. Product SDS.
- A “skin-safe” or food-contact badge on a cleaner is not dermatological approval.

## Deep dives (optional)

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Oxidation, ozone, and UV morphology</summary>

Three oxidative modes run at once: chain scission (soft, tacky face; tensile loss), crosslinking (harden or embrittle), and chemical alteration (solubility change). Oxygen attack is autocatalytic. Light accelerates it.

Natural rubber is cis-1,4-polyisoprene. Ozone attacks the double bond.

| Mode | Orientation / extent |
| --- | --- |
| Ozone | Cracks perpendicular to film stress; generally a wide area |
| Flex | Confined to the area under the greatest strain |
| UV | No preferred direction; surface crazing |

Handbook ambient ozone in that discussion is about 1–8 pphm. Sources include motors, lightning, and urban ultraviolet light plus nitrogen oxide.

ASTM D1149 estimates ozone cracking of vulcanized rubber under static or dynamic surface tensile strain in a chamber. Sunlight is excluded from that method. Chamber hours are not an outdoor-life prediction.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Warehouse heat and packaging UV</summary>

Ambient temperature in a non-air-conditioned warehouse can run 35–60 °C from floor to ceiling. The oxidation rate of latex film doubles for each 8.3 °C rise. Goods stored against the ceiling can oxidize about eight times as fast as similar goods at floor level.

UV-absorber packaging film may cost less than loading absorbers into the bulk compound. That is a plant cost note, not a closet SKU.

Handbook example: latex girdles rolled (no sharp edges) into cardboard tubes.

Freudenberg vendor alignment (not a standard): below 25 °C; polyethylene or aluminum packaging cues.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Wax bloom and static ozone barriers</summary>

<details>
<summary>What does phr mean?</summary>

**phr** means **parts per hundred rubber**: how much of an ingredient is added relative to 100 parts of dry rubber. Numbers below are handbook examples, not home recipes.

</details>

Most latex products in the handbook use 1–2.0 phr total antioxidant. For copper (or other metal) protection of NR, the example is 2 phr total: 1 phr against oxidation and 1 phr against copper.

Wax emulsion at 0.5–1.0 phr is used as a **static** ozone barrier after a continuous bloom forms. Flex or stretch cracks that bloom. Protection then fails on a short clock. Handbook example hours-to-fail:

| Condition | Hours (handbook example) |
| --- | ---: |
| No wax | 16 |
| VANWAX H | 76 |
| VANWAX H Special | 98 |
| VANWAX H Special, film flexed | 26 |

Substituted *p*-phenylenediamines are for **dynamic** ozone. They stain dark brick red and are restricted to very dark or black goods. Below 2 phr they do not protect well; 3 phr is often used.

Amine-type antioxidants stain. Phenolic antioxidants are the usual choice in latex when color matters. Wax bloom can block other migrants. None of those loadings is a polish you rub on a finished garment.

</details>
