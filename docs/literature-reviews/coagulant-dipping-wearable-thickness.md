# Coagulant Dipping for Wearable Thickness

> **Safety.** Acetic coagulants need PPE — add acid to water, not the reverse. Warm leach is required; no copper or brass fittings in dip or leach contact. See [Allergy and Skin Contact](/literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact) for skin-contact literacy.

## Executive summary

When straight multi-dip builds film too slowly, industrial practice moves to **coagulant dipping**: coat the former with a coacervant, dwell in compounded latex, withdraw a gelled shell. A single coagulant dip typically yields **0.2–0.8 mm** dry thickness — far more per pass than straight dipping (~0.01–0.05 mm). Films above roughly **8 mil** dry usually use coagulant for economics.

Primary levers are [coacervant strength](#coacervant-families), [dwell time](#dwell-thickness) (deposit often follows square-root-of-time kinetics), compound viscosity, and withdrawal speed. Follow with warm-water leach and [humidity-aware dry/cure](#leach-humidity). Use this after [Former Dip vs Mould Cast vs Flat Spread](/literature-reviews/former-dip-vs-mould-cast-vs-flat-spread) confirms liquid latex work and you need wearable gauge in fewer passes.

---

## When coagulant beats straight multi-dip {#when-coagulant}

Straight dips deposit a thin dry layer per pass. Coagulant destabilizes a thick gel layer in one dwell — a time-and-gauge trade, not automatic superiority without leach and stability discipline.

<details>
<summary>What is a coacervant?</summary>

A **coacervant** is a chemical coating on the former that makes liquid latex gel on contact. **Dry coacervants** (calcium chloride, calcium nitrate, cyclohexylammonium acetate) predominate — solvent evaporates before the latex dip. **Wet coacervants** (e.g. acetic acid) are less common and appear in some chloroprene or fabric-lined glove contexts. Plant formulations exist in handbooks as industrial examples, not cosplay recipes.

</details>

---

## Coacervant families {#coacervant-families}

Dry calcium salts are the default. Wet acid coacervants need extra PPE discipline. Match coacervant family to your compound and former material — do not copy glove-line percentages without checking wet-gel strength.

---

## Dwell time and thickness {#dwell-thickness}

Deposit thickness often rises with **square root of dwell time** until the coacervant is depleted. Higher compound viscosity increases deposit. Long dwell can leave a strong inner gel with a weak outer fluid layer — flow-off on withdrawal matters.

<details>
<summary>What is wet gel strength?</summary>

**Wet gel strength** is how well the gelled latex holds together before cure. Plants track it with **Chloroform Coagulant Number** — roughly 2–3 is the stable window. Below ~2, the deposit is "blown" — soft and collapsible on withdrawal.

</details>

---

## Stability, pinholes, and air {#stability-pinholes}

Entrapped air causes **pinholes** — reject-grade in industrial QC. Mild vacuum on storage containers; do not entrain air when filling the dip tank. Weak wet gel usually traces to Chloroform Number or coacervant strength, not only salt percentage.

---

## Leach, humidity, and second dip {#leach-humidity}

Warm-water leach is required after coagulant dips — it improves cured properties and reduces discoloration. Ideal plant conditions run **45–50% RH** at moderate temperature. Too high: retained moisture depresses stress and tensile. Too low: skin-dry surface and poor second-dip lamination. Chloroprene films need more ambient dry than natural rubber before a second dip.

---

## Wearable vs medical QC {#wearable-qc}

Medical glove hole tests certify dipped gloves, not fashion castings. Pinholes still matter in thin liquid-latex film — but the certification protocol is not your wearable QC checklist.

---

## Decision cues {#decision-cues}

```text
Target dry > ~0.2 mm one pass? → consider coagulant branch
Have warm leach + humidity control? → if no, fix environment before raising coacervant %
Weak wet gel / blown deposit? → check Chloroform No., not only Ca salt %
Second dip planned? → control first-film moisture (not skin-dry)
```

For defect names when things go wrong, see [Film Defect Atlas](/literature-reviews/film-defect-atlas-industrial-to-craft).
