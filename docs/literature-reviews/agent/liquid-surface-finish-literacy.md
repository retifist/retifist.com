# Liquid Film Surface Finish

Human page: https://retifist.com/literature-reviews/liquid-surface-finish-literacy

> **Safety.** Halogenation uses corrosive oxidizers. Parameters below are industrial vocabulary. Process: [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control).

## Executive summary

Gloss and matte on dipped or cast NR film usually come from the tool face the film dried against. Glazed former → shiny, vibrant color; bisque or sandblasted former → dull, flat color.[^1] Tack may be cut temporarily (talc) or permanently (aqueous halogenation).[^2] Bloom is a migrating wax layer, not shop dirt.[^3][^4]

Use this when [forming film](/literature-reviews/liquid-former-dip-vs-mould-cast-vs-flat-spread) and reading the resulting face, or when stored film looks powdery. Storage: [Aging, Storage and Care](/literature-reviews/liquid-aging-storage-and-care).

## Former finish sets the face

Porcelain former surface controls appearance: glaze yields shiny appearance and vibrant colors; bisque/sandblast yields dull, flat colors.[^1] On a flat cast or spread, the bed is the analog: contacting-face gloss copies tool polish. Forming routes: [Former Dip vs Mould Cast vs Flat Spread](/literature-reviews/liquid-former-dip-vs-mould-cast-vs-flat-spread). Bulk color in the compound is a separate lever: [Pigment and Filler Decks](/literature-reviews/liquid-pigment-filler-decks).

---

## Tack: talc or halogenation

Cured dipped film is often tacky. Talc is temporary industrial dusting. Permanent de-tack uses aqueous chlorine water, sodium hypochlorite, or bromine water in a tumbler; then neutralize, rinse, dry warm. Handbook example: ~0.15% Cl, 4.5–7.5 min at room temperature. No copper or brass in chlorine contact.[^2]

Process detail: [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control). Chlorinated glove surfaces show identifiable chlorine surface modification.[^5] Embossing alone is insufficient for wet grip; halogenation is the industrial class.[^2]

---

## Bloom after storage

Microcrystalline wax at 0.5–1.0 phr blooms to a static ozone barrier. Flex/stretch breaks the barrier.[^3] Peer work treats bloom as a migrating layer, not talc.[^4] Wiping can return tack if bloom was the only ozone shield. Compounding: [Antidegradants in DIY Compounds](/literature-reviews/liquid-antidegradants-diy-compounds).

## Reading cues

| Observation | First reading |
| --- | --- |
| Shiny face, vivid color | Glazed / smooth tool.[^1] |
| Dull, flat color | Bisque, sandblast, or rough bed.[^1] |
| Powder after storage | Wax bloom.[^3] |
| Still tacky after talc | Permanent class is halogenation.[^2] |
| Texture but poor wet grip | Embossing ≠ halogenation substitute.[^2] |

---

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Chlorination window (pointer)</summary>

Saturated chlorine water ~0.56% Cl, diluted to ~0.15% for treatment. Typical 4.5–7.5 min room-temperature tumble; neutralize; three rinses; dry 40–46°C.[^2] Full solution choice and rinse discipline: [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Wax bloom timing</summary>

Allow bloom time before ozone exposure. Handbook example: weeks at 23°C / 50% RH in kraft. Dynamic wear defeats wax. PPD antiozonants are a staining class for flexed dark goods.[^3]

</details>
