# Allergy and Skin Contact

Human page: /literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact

> **Not medical advice.** This article is literacy: names for reaction *classes*, typical *timing patterns*, and what labels do or do not mean. It is not diagnosis, treatment, emergency protocol, or a product “safe to wear” certificate. Seek a qualified clinician for symptoms.

## Executive summary

“Latex allergy” covers three patterns: immediate reaction to **proteins** in natural rubber latex (NRL); delayed contact reaction to **cure chemicals**; and **irritant** dermatitis, which is not that immune allergy. Collapsing them leads to the wrong avoidance and to false safety from “hypoallergenic,” “FDA,” or “food-contact” talk.

Medical **devices** that contain NRL must carry a specific caution and must not say “hypoallergenic” on that NRL device.[^1] Fashion goods typically sit outside that rule. Food-contact rubber tables list accelerators for **food**; they are not dermal wear clearance.[^2]

---

## Three reaction classes

NIOSH’s 1997 Alert on NRL gloves and other NRL products separates three patterns:[^3]

| Class | What the source is talking about | Typical timing *pattern* |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Irritant contact dermatitis | Skin irritation from gloves, washing, powder; **not** true allergy | Often during or soon after exposure. NIOSH does not pin that clock. |
| Delayed / Type IV-like (allergic contact dermatitis) | Reaction to **processing chemicals**, not rubber proteins | NIOSH: usually **24–48 h**.[^3] AAAAI: often **12–36 h**, usually added chemicals.[^9] Windows overlap. |
| Immediate / Type I (NRL protein) | Immune reaction to **NRL proteins** in previously sensitized people | Minutes to hours; can include a severe immediate (anaphylaxis) pattern.[^9] |

NRL **proteins** can cause allergic reactions; **processing chemicals** can cause skin rashes; synthetics called “latex” do **not** release those NRL proteins.[^3] OSHA’s hospital eTool uses the same three-way split and warns: do **not** assume hypoallergenic or powderless gloves are non-latex.[^4] OSHA’s 2008 SHIB points back to NIOSH 97-135 and notes NRL beyond exam gloves; still a workplace frame.[^5]

Clinical reviews: Hevea proteins → Type I; processing chemicals / antioxidants → Type IV.[^6][^7][^8] AAAAI glossary: immune overreaction to **proteins** in NRL; synthetic “latex” paints not shown to pose that protein hazard to latex-sensitive individuals.[^10]

Ammonia odor on concentrate can cause **human discomfort** (especially above 0.3% ammonia in the Practical Guide’s wording) and is not Type I or Type IV in that composition discussion.[^11] Fume handling: [Liquid latex ventilation and ammonia](/literature-reviews/liquid-ventilation-ammonia).

## What is in the film

Fresh NRL: rubber 30–35%; **proteins 1–1.5%**, plus resins, carbohydrates, minerals, water. Rubber particle surface covered by a **protein-lipid envelope**; lutoid membranes are protein.[^11] Historical 1932 picture: adsorbed protein layer / exterior protein shell, important in acid coagulation — composition, not allergy medicine.[^12]

ASTM D6499 significance statement (method class): Type I from HNRL **proteins** vs Type IV from **chemical additives**.[^13]

### Accelerator families

Organic accelerators plus zinc oxide enable fast latex-film cure; many are active below 100 °C.[^14] Families: thiuram, dithiocarbamate, xanthate, thiazole (including sulfenamide), thiourea, guanidine. Dithiocarbamates fastest and most used for latex; thiazoles and thiurams too slow alone, used as secondaries.[^14] Practical Guide names ZDEC (ZEDC), ZMBT, TMTM, tetraethylthiuram disulfide, DPTD.[^11]

Clinical Type IV lists match those **families**: carbamates, thiuram mix, 2-MBT, 1,3-diphenylguanidine;[^7] thiurams, carbamates, mercaptobenzothiazoles.[^8] Historical thiuram glove ACD; shift toward dithiocarbamates; thiuram↔dithiocarbamate cross-reactivity.[^15]

Blackley §16.3.7: dermatitis from certain vulcanization accelerators and antioxidants; a localized reaction can generalize. Gloves and condoms named for intimate skin contact — industrial/clinical frame, not fashion epidemiology.[^23] Wilson patch tests on 42 clinic patients (neat accelerators; reported via Blackley, not a standalone BJD fetch): dipentamethylenethiuram disulphide 30, tetramethylthiuram disulphide 20, 2-mercaptobenzthiazole 15, zinc diethyldithiocarbamate 2.[^23] Not garment incidence.

Some low-ammonia (LATZ) concentrate uses 0.2% ammonia + 0.0125% each TMTD and ZnO + 0.05% lauric acid.[^11] HPL Vol. 3 flags low-ammonia NR with a thiuram sulphide or dithiocarbamate secondary preservative as an example to avoid when designing goods for human contact (nitrosamine-reformulation lane).[^23]

Representative ZDEC SDS (CAS 14324-55-1): GHS Skin Sens. 1; H317 “May cause an allergic skin reaction.” Neat-chemical hazard class, not diagnosis of a worn garment.[^17]

## What labels mean

21 CFR **801.437** is medical-device NRL labeling. Scope: devices that contain natural rubber latex contacting humans. Proteins named in (a). Required bold caution: this product contains natural rubber latex which may cause allergic reactions. **(h) the labeling shall not contain the term “hypoallergenic.”** Noncompliance is misbranding under (j).[^1]

1997 final rule: caution on medical devices/packaging containing NRL or dry natural rubber contacting humans; hypoallergenicity claims removed because Draize-type tests were inappropriate for protein sensitivity.[^18] OSHA eTool cites 801.437 in the hospital-glove setting.[^4]

Fashion goods typically sit outside that device rule. Absence of a device-style NRL caution is not evidence the product is protein-free or accelerator-free. “Hypoallergenic” on fashion is not 801.437 compliance and not a Type I / Type IV clearance.

FDA banned powdered surgeon’s / patient-exam gloves and absorbable lubricating powder; NRL allergen on powder among named risks. Non-powdered NRL gloves still use §801.437.[^19] NIOSH: powder binds proteins.[^3]

## Food-contact FDA is not wear certification

21 CFR **177.2600** is rubber articles intended for repeated use in producing, packing, or holding **food**. Accelerator listings and a total cap (not to exceed 1.5% by weight of rubber product); extractive limits; cleanse-before-first-food-use.[^2] Vanderbilt reprints the same food-contact identity map; antidegradants under a separate cap (example: as antioxidant, not to exceed 5% by weight of rubber product).[^14] None of those rows is a dermal wear or allergy certificate.

Clinical Type IV to antioxidants is an external literature topic (Nucera lists antioxidants among Type IV contactants).[^7]

## Buyer questions

Do **not** DIY patch-test a garment or bottle on skin.

| Ask | Why it matters | What silence means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Natural rubber latex (Hevea NRL), or a synthetic called “latex”? | NRL proteins are the Type I story; synthetics named “latex” do not release those proteins[^3][^10] | Cannot rule NRL in or out |
| Device-style NRL caution (801.437)? | Required for in-scope **devices**[^1] | Absence on fashion goods ≠ protein-free |
| Listing says **hypoallergenic**? | Banned on NR **devices** under 801.437(h);[^1] fashion = unregulated marketing | Not a Type I/IV clearance |
| Accelerator families named, or SDS available? | Type IV literacy is chemical families;[^7] SDS H317 is class, not diagnosis[^17] | Typical fashion silence |
| Protein method cited (D5712 vs D6499)? | Method classes; D6499 antigenic ≠ proven allergenic correlation[^21][^13] | Fashion numbers rarely cited |
| “FDA” or food-contact on the card? | 177.2600 is food, not skin[^2] | Not a wear cert |

ISO 12243 assays water-extractable protein in NR **medical gloves**; does not address safety or labelling; non-glove extraction not validated.[^20] A glove protein number is not a garment safe/unsafe score.

Oils that attack film: [Sheet aging, storage and care](/literature-reviews/sheet-aging-storage-and-care), [Liquid latex aging, storage and care](/literature-reviews/liquid-aging-storage-and-care), [Compatibility matrix](/literature-reviews/compatibility-matrix-metals-oils-plastics).

## When to stop and seek care

Stop wearing or handling the article and seek a qualified clinician if you notice a new rash, hives, swelling, breathing difficulty, or any reaction you worry about after latex contact. Do not “challenge” the garment to confirm. Do not treat chlorination, shine, or a different oil as a cure.

Vanderbilt families:[^14] thiuram, dithiocarbamate, xanthate, thiazole including sulfenamide, thiourea, guanidine. HPL Table 16.6 names zinc/sodium xanthates among the fastest latex sulfur-vulcanization accelerators.[^23]

| 2-Mercaptobenzthiazole (MBT) | 15 |
| Zinc diethyldithiocarbamate (ZDEC class) | 2 |

Surgeon-glove example (handbook, not craft recipe):[^11] 50% ZDEC/ZDBC 0.50–0.85 phr; 50% ZMBT 0.25–0.45 phr.

21 CFR 177.2600: accelerators total not to exceed 1.5% by weight of rubber product.[^2][^14] Food-contact, not dermal clearance.

<summary>Deep dive: Protein measurement method classes</summary>

| Method | What it reports | What it does not do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ASTM D5712 (modified Lowry) | Total aqueous extractable protein | Not allergen-specific; total protein ≠ clinical potency[^21] |
| ASTM D6499 | Antigenic (not necessarily allergenic) Hevea protein | Not a pass/fail “safe garment”; allergenic correlation not fully established[^13] |

Practical Guide Category 4: less than 200 µg total protein per gram dry weight of latex.[^11]

Glove process: post-leach to minimize residual protein; defect = increased protein from insufficient leaching / high-protein latex.[^11]

ASTM D3578 (exam gloves): protein and antigenic protein among conformance tests.[^22]
