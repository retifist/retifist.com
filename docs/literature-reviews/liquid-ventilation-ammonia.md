# Liquid Latex Ventilation and Ammonia

> **Safety.** This is chemical-hygiene literacy, not a legal compliance certificate for your jurisdiction. Ammonia and rubber chemicals can irritate or sensitize. If you feel dizzy, nauseated, or get a new rash or breathing symptoms, stop work and get fresh air. Seek medical advice as appropriate.

## Executive summary

Liquid latex work means **ammonia-preserved latex** and, if you compound, **dry powders**. The latex itself is water-based. The documented shop hazards are prolonged ammonia fumes and powder sensitization, plus acid/alkali splash discipline.

Use this before you open a bottle indoors, and before you weigh zinc oxide or accelerator powder. ## Ammonia fumes {#ammonia-fumes}

Vanderbilt’s laboratory health section requires **ventilation**. It notes that **prolonged ammonia fumes are hazardous** and prefers **low-ammonia latex plus ventilation** when ammonia is the driver.

Workplace ammonia numbers (CAS 7664-41-7): OSHA PEL 50 ppm; NIOSH REL 25 ppm / STEL 35 ppm; IDLH 300 ppm. Those are occupational limits, not a hobby “hours per week” budget.

Compound on a wettable, clean surface with ventilation when the odor is strong, especially with high-ammonia concentrate types. Open bottles in ventilated space. That is not a recipe to air out concentrate until it spoils.

The Practical Guide notes human discomfort from ammonia odor, especially above 0.3% ammonia in that composition discussion. Discomfort is not Type I or Type IV. See [Allergy and Skin Contact](/literature-reviews/allergy-and-skin-contact).

## Powders and splash {#powders-and-splash}

The same lab section requires **respirators when dumping powders**, wash skin, and acknowledges **rubber-chemical sensitization**.

Weigh zinc oxide, sulfur, accelerators, and antioxidants with local exhaust or a respirator rated for fine dust per the supplier SDS. Do not put your face over an open jar because the polymer is “natural.”

<details>
<summary>What is an SDS?</summary>

An **SDS** (Safety Data Sheet) is the manufacturer’s official hazard document. For each latex and each compounding powder, the SDS lists irritation, sensitization, and handling. Class hazards beat brand folklore.

</details>

**Always add acid or alkali to water, never the reverse.** Keep acids and alkalis segregated.

## Metal hygiene {#metal-hygiene}

Lab storage repeats the bulk-latex metal bans: **never copper alloys; avoid galvanized** fittings that contact latex. A brass hose barb or a galvanized tap on the liquid is the plant-scale example. Storage detail: [Liquid latex aging, storage and care](/literature-reviews/liquid-aging-storage-and-care).

---

## Shop rules {#shop-rules}

1. Read the SDS for the exact latex and each compounding powder.
2. Keep liquid latex off copper, brass, and galvanized contact.
3. Closed bottles when you are not pouring.
4. Laundry work clothes that saw powder.
5. Do not eat in the active work zone.

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Lab compounding order (plant bench, not a home card)</summary>

Incoming latex QC in that chapter includes total solids (ASTM D1076), viscosity, pH, mechanical stability, metals, and ammonia titration.

Addition order while stirring: stabilizers → zinc oxide / sulfur / antioxidant / accelerators → fillers / colors → thickeners.

Evaluation films: spread on glass or dip; leach before cure 20–30 min at 40–50 °C, then air dry. Named aging tests (heat tube, air/oxygen bomb, light/weather, ozone) are plant QC, not home-shop requirements.
