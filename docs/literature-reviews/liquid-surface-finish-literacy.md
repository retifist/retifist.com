# Liquid Film Surface Finish

> **Safety.** Halogenation uses corrosive oxidizers. The numbers below are industrial vocabulary, not a home recipe. Process detail lives in [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control).

## Executive summary

On dipped or cast film, gloss and matte usually come from the surface the film dried against. A **glazed** former gives a shiny face and vivid color. A **bisque** or sandblasted former gives a dull, flat face. Tack can be cut for a while with talc, or permanently by halogenation. **Bloom** is wax moving to the surface.

Use this when you are [forming film](/literature-reviews/liquid-former-dip-vs-mould-cast-vs-flat-spread) and reading the face you got, or when stored film looks powdery. Storage sits in [Aging, Storage and Care](/literature-reviews/liquid-aging-storage-and-care).

## Former finish sets the face {#former-finish}

<details>
<summary>What is a glazed vs bisque former?</summary>

A **glazed** former has a smooth, polished porcelain surface. Film that dries against it comes off shiny, with vibrant color. A **bisque** or sandblasted former has a micro-rough face. Film comes off dull and flat. The finish is set while the film forms.

</details>

On a flat cast or spread, the bed does the same job as the former: the contacting face copies the tool. A polished bed reads glossier than a rough one. Forming geometry is in [Former Dip vs Mould Cast vs Flat Spread](/literature-reviews/liquid-former-dip-vs-mould-cast-vs-flat-spread).

Bulk color in the compound is a different lever than this face finish. See [Pigment and Filler Decks](/literature-reviews/liquid-pigment-filler-decks).

## Tack: talc or halogenation {#tack}

Fresh cured film is often tacky. Plants dust with **talc** for handling. That dusting is temporary.

Permanent de-tack on dipped goods is aqueous **halogenation**: chlorine water, sodium hypochlorite, or bromine water, usually in a tumbler, then neutralize, rinse, and dry warm. Handbook example: about 0.15% chlorine, 4.5–7.5 minutes at room temperature. No copper or brass in contact with the chlorine liquor.

That process belongs in [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control). Chlorinated glove surfaces show a chemically modified, chlorine-bearing skin.

## Bloom after storage {#bloom}

<details>
<summary>What is wax bloom?</summary>

**Wax bloom** is antidegradant wax moving to the rubber surface. Industrial compounds use microcrystalline wax at 0.5–1.0 **phr** to build a surface film that protects against **static** ozone. Flexing and stretching crack that film. The powder can look like chalk. Peer work on NR bloom treats it as a migrating layer, not leftover talc.

</details>

<details>
<summary>What does phr mean?</summary>

**phr** is parts per hundred rubber. 1.0 phr means one part of that ingredient for every 100 parts rubber in the compound.

</details>

Wiping the powder off can bring tack back if the bloom was the only ozone shield. Wax bloom is for film that sits still. Compounding choices for antidegradants sit in [Antidegradants in DIY Compounds](/literature-reviews/liquid-antidegradants-diy-compounds).

## Reading cues {#cues}

| What you see | Think first |
| --- | --- |
| Shiny face, vivid color | Glazed or smooth tool. |
| Dull, flat color | Bisque, sandblast, or rough bed. |
| Powder after storage | Wax bloom. |
| Still tacky after talc | Halogenation is the permanent class. |
| Texture but poor wet grip | Embossing alone is not enough. Halogenation is the industrial wet-grip class. |

## Deep dives (optional)

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Chlorination window (pointer)</summary>

Saturated chlorine water is about 0.56% chlorine, diluted to about 0.15% for treatment. Typical tumble is 4.5–7.5 minutes at room temperature, then neutralize, triple rinse, and dry at 40–46°C. Full solution choice and rinse discipline: [Chlorination for Tack Control](/literature-reviews/liquid-chlorination-tack-control).

</details>

<details>
<summary>Deep dive: Wax bloom timing</summary>

Industrial guidance allows bloom time before ozone exposure. One handbook example: weeks at 23°C and 50% relative humidity in kraft wrapping. Dynamic wear defeats the wax barrier. PPD antiozonants are a different class; they can stain dark goods.

</details>
